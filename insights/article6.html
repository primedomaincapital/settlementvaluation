How Much Is a Personal Injury Case Worth? Settlement Ranges by Injury Type

The average personal injury payout is $52,900 — and almost no case settles for it. Ranges by injury type, and the four factors that move a case inside its band.

Prime Domain Capital InsightsNovember 19, 2026

The question arrives on the first call, before the medical records are in, before liability is established, before anyone has seen a single bill. "What is my case worth?" The lawyer's honest answer is that the case is worth what a jury would award minus the risk that it will not, plus the cost of finding out — and none of those numbers exist yet. But the client is not asking for a formula. They are asking for a range, a number they can hold on to while the treatment continues and the bills pile up. The ranges exist. They are wider than anyone likes, and they are more useful than anyone admits.

What the averages actually say

The most quoted benchmark comes from Martindale-Nolo Research, whose 2017 reader survey of personal injury claimants found an average payout of $52,900. The average is misleading in the usual way: more than half of the readers who received money collected between $3,000 and $25,000, and only 15 percent recovered more than $75,000. The median is far below the average, because a small number of large recoveries pull the mean upward. The same survey found that readers who hired a lawyer averaged $77,600, against $17,600 for those who represented themselves. Readers who negotiated for a better offer averaged $42,500, against $11,800 for those who accepted the first offer. Readers who filed a lawsuit or gave notice of their intent averaged $45,500, against $23,000 for those who did not.

Federal court data tells a similar story at a higher level. Westlaw Edge analyzed 745 federal personal injury cases from 2013 through 2022 that ended with a settlement or award and found a median settlement of $75,000 — but more than 40 percent of plaintiffs in that sample received $5,000 or less. The Bureau of Justice Statistics, in its civil justice survey of state courts, put the median jury award at $24,000. The numbers disagree with each other because they measure different things: reader surveys, federal settlements, state jury awards. The one thing they agree on is the shape of the distribution — most cases are small, a few are very large, and the average describes almost no individual case.

Ranges by injury type

The ranges by severity are the numbers clients actually use, compiled from practitioner settlement data published by PersonalInjuryLaw.org. Minor injuries — soft tissue, sprains, brief treatment, no surgery — typically settle between $3,000 and $25,000. Moderate injuries — fractures, surgery, months of treatment, some permanent limitation — typically settle between $25,000 and $100,000. Severe injuries — multiple surgeries, permanent disability, significant wage loss — typically settle between $100,000 and $500,000. Catastrophic injuries — traumatic brain injury, spinal cord injury, amputation, wrongful death — typically settle from $500,000 to $25 million and above. These are practitioner benchmarks, not guarantees, and the spread inside each band is driven by factors the injury label does not capture.

Motor vehicle cases, the largest single category, illustrate the point. Based on actual insurance claims paid nationwide, the 2022 average bodily injury payment was $28,950, ranging from about $13,700 in the lowest-amount state to $43,400 in the highest. Michigan, at more than $77,600, is an outlier because of its no-fault insurance law. The same accident, the same injuries, in two different states: a threefold difference in the check.

What moves a case inside its band

Four factors move a case more than the injury label does. The first is liability clarity: a rear-end collision with a police report is worth more than a disputed-liability slip and fall with the same injuries, because the defense cannot credibly threaten a zero verdict. The second is the venue: Sedgwick's Summer 2026 report shows settlement severity growing 12.6 percent a year while verdict severity grows 3.7 percent, and the gap is widest in the venues where nuclear verdicts are most common — Texas, where social inflation ran 18 percent in the second quarter of 2026, and Florida, at 17 percent. The third is the insurance limit: a $25,000 policy is a $25,000 case, however severe the injury, unless the defendant has assets worth pursuing. The fourth is the plaintiff: a credible, consistent, sympathetic plaintiff is worth more than an identical injury attached to a plaintiff the jury will not believe.

The venue effect deserves its own paragraph, because it is the factor clients understand least and lawyers price most. Social inflation — the term the insurance industry uses for the rise in jury awards driven by changing attitudes toward corporations and institutions — ran 18 percent in Texas and 17 percent in Florida in the second quarter of 2026, according to Enlyte's discussion with Aon's Kevin Combes. The American Transportation Research Institute put the median nuclear verdict at $51 million in 2024, with 135 nuclear verdicts totaling $31.3 billion in a single year. A moderate-injury case in a venue where juries routinely return eight-figure awards settles for more than the identical injury in a venue where they do not — not because the injury is worth more, but because the defense is pricing the tail. The range for the injury is national. The price for the case is local.

The funding question matters too. Sedgwick's data shows litigation funding in auto liability cases growing 44 percent a year since 2022, and funded cases run 9.6 times longer than unfunded ones. A funded case will demand more and wait longer — and the duration itself is part of the cost, because three years of 12.6 percent severity growth takes a $300,000 average settlement to roughly $430,000.

The counterargument: ranges mislead more than they help

There is a serious argument against publishing ranges at all. A client who reads that moderate injuries settle for $25,000 to $100,000 will anchor on the top of the range, and a lawyer who quotes the range without the caveats is setting up a disappointment. The ranges also hide the most important number in any individual case: the net. A $100,000 settlement with $40,000 in medical liens, a 33 percent fee, and $15,000 in costs leaves the client with less than a $50,000 settlement with no liens at all. The gross number is the one that gets quoted. The net number is the one that gets spent.

The ranges are also stale the moment they are printed. Settlement severity is growing at 12.6 percent a year, which means a range published in 2024 understates 2026 values by more than a quarter. A valuation built on last year's comparables is a valuation built on last year's market.

What this means for valuing a case

Use the ranges as a starting point, not an answer. The right question is not "what do moderate injuries settle for" but "what does this injury settle for, in this venue, against this defendant, with these limits, for this plaintiff." Price the case on comparables from the same jurisdiction in the last two years, not on national averages from a 2017 survey. Discount for liability risk, discount for the plaintiff's credibility, and add for the venue's verdict history. And value the net, not the gross: liens, fees, and costs are not footnotes. They are the difference between a settlement that changes a client's life and one that merely pays the bills.

The average personal injury settlement is $52,900. Almost no case settles for $52,900. That is not a contradiction. It is the whole point.
