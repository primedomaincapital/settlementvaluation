The Fear Premium: Settlements Are Growing Four Times Faster Than Verdicts

Settlement severity is growing four times faster than verdict severity — because cases are priced by what juries might do, not by what they do.

Prime Domain Capital InsightsNovember 9, 2026

The settlement conference lasted twenty minutes. The demand was $2.4 million. The defense had authority to $900,000. The gap was not about the injuries, the medical bills, or the liability — both sides agreed on all of that. The gap was about a verdict that would never happen. The plaintiff's lawyer was pricing the one-in-eighty chance of a nuclear jury award. The defense was pricing the seventy-five percent chance that, if the case ever reached a jury, the verdict would come in below the last demand. They were not negotiating the same case. They were negotiating two different fears — and the settlement that eventually closed, at $1.6 million, was priced by the fear, not the facts. That is how settlement valuation works in 2026.

Settlements are growing four times faster than verdicts

Sedgwick's Summer 2026 Liability Litigation Observations and Trends report, published June 17, 2026 and authored by chief claims officer Max Koonce and vice president of liability practice Steve Ellis, puts the split in numbers. Settlement severity in Sedgwick's bodily injury portfolio grew at an average annual rate of 12.6 percent from 2021 to 2025, while verdict severity grew at 3.7 percent over the same period — roughly tracking inflation. A four-to-one differential sustained across five years is not noise. It is a structural feature of how litigated claims are now valued inside retained layers. Sedgwick names the dynamic directly: "nuclear fallout settlements." Cases are priced not by what juries find but by what carriers and defense counsel fear juries might do.

The data shows the fear is consistently overpriced. In 2025, only 1.25 percent of bodily injury litigated cases in Sedgwick's portfolio went to verdict, down sharply from approximately 12 percent in 2016. Of the cases that did reach a jury, 75.4 percent came in below the last settlement demand made before trial. In more than three out of four tried cases, the nuclear outcome that drove the settlement negotiation never materialized. The distribution tells the same story from the other side: 50.3 percent of settled cases exceeded $500,000, while only 31.5 percent of verdicted cases cleared that threshold. Settlements are systematically priced above the verdicts they are meant to avoid.

The funding engine behind the fear

Third-party litigation funding is the structural driver that makes the fear durable. Sedgwick's data shows the use of litigation funding in auto liability has accelerated with an average annual increase of 44 percent since 2022, with 2026 already on target to exceed that pace. General liability funding grew more modestly, at 18 percent a year since 2022, and is trending below prior-year levels in 2026. In 2025, 4.6 percent of closed auto bodily injury litigated claims involved some form of third-party funding; year-to-date in 2026 that has risen to 5.1 percent. The effect on duration is dramatic: litigated auto bodily injury cases with third-party funding run 9.6 times longer than those without, and general liability funded cases average 11.1 times longer. New York and Georgia account for 82 percent of all funded cases in Sedgwick's data. Funding does not just extend cases — it changes who can afford to wait, and waiting is what turns a $300,000 average settlement into roughly $430,000 after three years of compounding at current severity growth.

The verdicts themselves remain extreme at the tail. The American Transportation Research Institute's December 2025 report, Trucking Litigation: A Forensic Analysis, analyzed 454 truck tort cases concluded between 2019 and 2024 and found the median nuclear verdict reached $36 million in 2022, roughly 50 percent higher than the 2013 median. By 2024, one industry tracker counted 135 nuclear verdicts totaling $31.3 billion — a 52 percent increase in frequency and a 116 percent increase in total value from the prior year, with the median climbing to $51 million. In March 2026, a Utah jury awarded $81 million to the family of 12-year-old Michael Madsen, struck and killed by a commercial truck in a crosswalk in 2018 — the largest verdict in Utah history, against Beacon Roofing Supply and its driver. Second-quarter 2026 verdict data put social inflation at 18 percent in Texas and 17 percent in Florida. Enlyte's September 2026 discussion with Aon's Kevin Combes describes casualty severity undergoing structural change, with large verdicts becoming far more frequent and the upper end of the severity distribution moving much faster than historical experience — driven by earlier attorney involvement, changing treatment patterns, litigation funding, and venue-by-venue regulatory differences.

The counterargument: the fear may be rational

Before dismissing the fear premium as pure overpayment, consider what the tail actually contains. ATRI's analysis found that for high-stakes cases worth more than $5 million, settlements tended to be lower than jury verdicts — carriers that settled avoided even larger payouts at trial. The median award for verdicts above $1 million was $3.6 million in state courts, against $2.5 million in federal courts. The 25 percent of tried cases that do exceed the last demand are the ones that destroy reserves, and a self-insured retention layer absorbs the full loss before excess coverage attaches. A risk manager who settles at a premium is not necessarily overpaying. They are buying certainty against a distribution whose worst outcomes are unbounded, in a venue where the jury has heard about the defendant's safety record for three weeks. The fear premium is insurance, and like all insurance it looks wasteful until the day it pays.

That defense has a limit, though. Insurance priced on fear rather than frequency eventually becomes its own cost driver: reserves set on nuclear-verdict probability inflate the settlement baseline, the inflated baseline becomes the next case's comparable, and the cycle compounds. Three years of 12.6 percent severity growth takes a $300,000 average settlement to roughly $430,000 — and the verdict data does not support the increase. The system is paying for a risk that materializes one time in eighty, at a price set as though it materializes one time in four.

What this means for valuing a case

Price the tail explicitly, and separately from the case. A settlement valuation that folds nuclear-verdict risk into a single number hides the most important judgment in the file: how likely the tail is, in this venue, with this defendant, in front of this jury pool. Track funding presence as a pricing input — a funded case will last longer and demand more, and the duration itself is part of the cost. And reserve on the settlement-severity trend, not the verdict trend, because the settlement trend is the one the money actually follows. The 97 percent statistic — that almost every case settles — was always true. What changed is what the settlement is priced against. It used to be priced against the verdict. Now it is priced against the fear of one. The firms that understand the difference will keep paying the premium when it is warranted and stop paying it when it is not. The firms that do not will keep funding the fear, one settlement at a time.
