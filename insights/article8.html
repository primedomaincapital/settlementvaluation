Mediation vs. Trial: What the Settlement Gap Really Costs

The mediation ended $200,000 apart. The plaintiff's lawyer recommended settling — and the client asked why, if they were right, they were paying to avoid trial.

Prime Domain Capital InsightsNovember 19, 2026

The mediation ended at 6 p.m. The demand had been $2.4 million. The defense had authority to $900,000. The mediator spent the last hour shuttling between rooms, carrying numbers that moved by smaller and smaller increments, until the gap was $200,000 and neither side would move again. The plaintiff's lawyer did the math: trial would cost $150,000 in expert fees alone, take eighteen months, and end with a jury that might award $3 million or might award nothing. He recommended settling at $1.6 million. His client asked the question every client asks at that moment: if we are right, why are we paying to avoid the trial? The answer is the most expensive lesson in litigation. Being right and being paid are two different things, and the gap between them is what mediation prices.

The decision error study

The most rigorous answer to that question comes from a study by Randall Kiser, published in the Journal of Empirical Legal Studies, which examined 2,054 cases that went to trial between 2002 and 2005. Kiser's finding, reported by Jonathan Glater in The New York Times in 2008, was blunt: plaintiffs who rejected a settlement and went to trial were wrong 61 percent of the time — the jury awarded less than the settlement they had turned down, or nothing at all. The average cost of the plaintiff's decision error was $43,100. Insurers were wrong less often, 24 percent of the time, but when they were wrong they were wrong catastrophically: the average cost of the insurer's decision error was $1,100,000. In just 15 percent of cases were both sides right to go to trial — meaning the defendant paid less than the plaintiff had wanted but the plaintiff got more than the defendant had offered. The other 85 percent of the time, at least one side would have been better off settling.

The study's sample is old, but its logic has only strengthened. The cases that reach trial are the cases where both sides are confident, and confidence is not evidence. The plaintiff's lawyer who turns down $1.6 million for a trial is betting that a jury of strangers will value the case higher than a professional adjuster who has seen a thousand like it. Sixty-one percent of the time, that bet loses.

What mediation actually delivers

Mediation is the mechanism that converts that bet into a price. The American Bar Association reports that mediations end in agreement 70 to 80 percent of the time, and the agreements hold: mediated settlements show higher long-term compliance than court judgments, because the parties helped craft the deal themselves. The Circuit Court of Cook County, which runs one of the largest court-annexed mediation programs in the country, reports that 50 to 65 percent of referred cases reach full or partial agreement at mediation — and many of the rest settle at a subsequent judicial conference, still avoiding trial. In California, practitioner guides report that more than 90 percent of personal injury cases resolve at or shortly after mediation.

The cost difference is stark. A mediation typically runs one day. Mediator fees for attorney-mediators generally run $3,000 to $8,000 per day; retired judges command $8,000 to $15,000 or more per day for high-value cases. A trial, by contrast, runs weeks, and the expert witness fees alone commonly run $75,000 to $150,000 through trial in a medical malpractice case, before anyone counts attorney time. The Mediation Group, a Boston-based provider, estimates that parties can spend up to 80 percent less by choosing mediation over litigation. The savings are not theoretical. They are the difference between a settlement that pays the client and a trial that pays the experts.

What trial actually costs

The trial's price is not just the money. It is the time and the variance. Sedgwick's Summer 2026 report found that only 1.25 percent of bodily injury litigated cases went to verdict in 2025, down from approximately 12 percent in 2016. Of the cases that did reach a jury, 75.4 percent came in below the last settlement demand made before trial. The verdicts that do happen are extreme at the tail: the median nuclear verdict reached $51 million in 2024, and one industry tracker counted 135 nuclear verdicts totaling $31.3 billion that year. But the tail is not the distribution. For high-stakes cases worth more than $5 million, the American Transportation Research Institute found that settlements tended to be lower than jury verdicts — carriers that settled avoided even larger payouts at trial. The trial is a lottery ticket with a very expensive entry fee, and most of the time the ticket loses.

The duration compounds the cost. Litigated auto bodily injury cases with third-party funding run 9.6 times longer than those without, according to Sedgwick, and general liability funded cases average 11.1 times longer. Three years of 12.6 percent severity growth takes a $300,000 average settlement to roughly $430,000 — and the plaintiff who waits three years for a trial is paying for that wait in interest, in medical bills, and in the years of their life spent inside a lawsuit.

The counterargument: sometimes the trial is the right call

None of this means mediation always wins. The 15 percent of cases where both sides were right to go to trial are real, and they share a profile: a liability dispute that genuinely turns on credibility, a damages question no comparable can answer, a defendant whose settlement authority is a fraction of the case's value because the carrier has decided to make an example of the claim. A plaintiff who accepts a lowball mediation offer in one of those cases has not saved money. They have given it away.

The insurer's 24 percent error rate is the other side of the same coin. When carriers get the value wrong, they get it wrong by an average of $1.1 million — which means the plaintiff who holds out in the right case is not being stubborn. They are being paid for the carrier's mistake. The Kiser study's lesson is not "always settle." It is "know which kind of case you are in, and price the trial accordingly."

What this means for valuing a case

Price the trial explicitly, and separately from the case. A settlement valuation that folds trial risk into a single number hides the most important judgment in the file: the probability that the jury beats the offer, and the cost of finding out. The Kiser numbers give the shape of that judgment — a 61 percent chance the plaintiff does worse at trial, an average cost of $43,100 when they do, against a 24 percent chance the carrier does worse, at an average cost of $1.1 million when it does. Those are not arguments for settling every case. They are the odds the settlement is priced against.

Second, mediate at the right time, with the right mediator, and with the file complete. A mediation held before the medical records are in, or before the expert has formed an opinion, is a mediation that leaves money on the table. A mediation held after the trial costs have already been sunk is a mediation that has lost its leverage. And third, treat the mediation offer as data, not as an insult. The gap between the demand and the offer is information about how the other side values the case — and in a market where 75.4 percent of tried cases come in below the last demand, the offer is usually closer to the verdict than the demand is.
